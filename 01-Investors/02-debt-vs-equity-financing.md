## Example: Raising $100 Through Debt-Financing
* An investor lends the company $100
  * The maturity date for the loan is 2 years
  * The interest on the loan becomes $10, resulting in $110 principles plus interest
* The lender does not ordinarily receive company ownership
* However, the company must repay the loan even if it performs poorly
* If the company fails, debt holders generally get paid before shareholders

## Example: Raising $100 Through Equity-Financing
* An investor gives the company $100 in exchange for shares
  * **T:X** means that the investment terms have to be negotiated
  * **Liq:A/IPO** refers to liquidation preference
    * This determines how proceeds are distributed if the company is sold or shut down
    * A “1×” liquidation preference would generally allow the investor to recover their original $100
    * Only the liquidation preference investor gets some form of gauranteed money back

## Example: 0 to $100 Million
* Shows the extreme upside of equity
* The shareholders collectively own the increase in a company from $0 to $100 million
* Owning 100% of the company would mean owning the entire $1 million of equity value

## Example: Convertible and SAFE Notes
* **Convertible Note:** begins as a loan but later converts into shares, usually when a startup raises a priced equity round
* **SAFE:** not traditional debt (no interest or maturity date), where the investor provides money now and receives shares during a future financing event. These notes were created by Carolynn Levy at the Y-Combinator, but spread to industry standard
* These instruments allow the startup to postpone determining its exact valuation and share price.
